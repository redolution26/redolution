Midlife and menopause

Hormonal change does not begin at 50. Your body moves through stages from your twenties onward, and knowing what to expect makes each one easier to prepare for.

Perimenopause is the transition. It often starts in the mid-40s and lasts about four years on average, but can last from two to ten. Menopause is the point when you have gone 12 months without a period, at 51 on average. Postmenopause is every year after that.

Ages are approximate, because every woman’s body keeps its own schedule. Use these stages as a guide, not a deadline. Treatments such as hormone therapy are not right for everyone, so talk to a doctor or nurse about your own situation.

At every age: move your body (strength training included), sleep well, eat enough protein, calcium and vitamin D, avoid smoking, go easy on alcohol, track your cycle and symptoms, and keep up your checkups.

25–30Your hormonal baseline

Estrogen and progesterone follow a fairly steady monthly pattern for many women.

What you may notice

Cycles are often regular, but PMS, cramps, acne or heavy bleeding can appear. Conditions like PCOS and endometriosis are often first noticed in these years.

Mindset

Learn what is normal for you. Knowing your usual pattern makes any change easier to spot, and your pain is worth taking seriously.

Lifestyle

Bones are still building and reach peak mass around age 30, so get calcium, vitamin D and weight-bearing exercise. Track your cycle and keep up regular checkups and screenings.

See a doctor if: periods are very painful, very heavy, or missing for months.

30–35The busy years

Hormones respond strongly to stress, sleep and life events.

What you may notice

Cycles often stay regular, but pregnancy, breastfeeding, stress, poor sleep or starting and stopping birth control can change them. Fertility begins a gradual decline in the early 30s. Thyroid problems are common in women and can affect energy, mood and cycles.

Mindset

Protect your energy. Rest is not a luxury, because your hormones notice when you are run down. If you hope to have children, getting information early is calming, not scary.

Lifestyle

Strength training twice a week, regular meals with protein and iron-rich foods, steady sleep, and a stress outlet you actually enjoy.

See a doctor if: you feel exhausted all the time, your cycles change suddenly, or you have not conceived after 12 months of trying (6 months if you are over 35).

35–40Subtle shifts

Egg supply and quality decline more quickly.

What you may notice

Some women notice shorter cycles, heavier periods or stronger PMS. Fibroids and heavy bleeding are common reasons to seek care. A few women begin perimenopause in their late 30s.

Mindset

Notice changes without fear. Most changes at this age have a cause that can be found and treated.

Lifestyle

Keep up strength training and sleep, eat iron-rich foods if your periods are heavy, limit alcohol and avoid smoking. Keep logging your cycle.

See a doctor if: you soak through a pad or tampon every hour or two, pass clots larger than a coin, or your periods become very irregular or stop before 40.

40–45The transition may begin

Perimenopause most often begins in the mid-40s. Estrogen starts to swing up and down.

What you may notice

Cycles may get shorter, longer, lighter or heavier. Hot flashes, night sweats, poor sleep, mood changes, brain fog and lower desire are common. Pregnancy is still possible.

Mindset

Swinging hormones are not a personal failing. These symptoms are real, common and treatable, and asking for help early is a strength.

Lifestyle

Log your symptoms (our log above can help). Do strength and weight-bearing exercise, eat protein, calcium and vitamin D, and keep a steady sleep routine. Cut back on alcohol or caffeine if they trigger hot flashes. Keep using contraception if you do not want to get pregnant.

See a doctor if: symptoms disrupt your daily life, bleeding is very heavy, or you bleed between periods.

45–50Perimenopause in full swing

Estrogen falls and swings more widely.

What you may notice

Periods may be skipped or far apart, then return. Hot flashes and night sweats are common, along with vaginal dryness, bladder changes, joint aches, and sleep and mood changes. Bone loss speeds up, and heart-health risks begin to rise.

Mindset

This is a transition, not an ending. Perimenopause lasts about four years on average, though it can be from two to ten. Treat it like any big life change: gather information, build support, and ask for what you need at home and work.

Lifestyle

Dress in layers and keep your bedroom cool. Add pelvic floor exercises to your strength training. Have your blood pressure, cholesterol and blood sugar checked, and ask about bone health and treatment options, including hormone and non-hormone choices.

See a doctor if: you bleed after sex or between periods, bleeding is very heavy, or low mood or anxiety is hard to manage.

50–55Menopause

Menopause means 12 months in a row without a period. The average age is 51, and anywhere from 45 to 55 is typical.

What you may notice

Hot flashes, night sweats, dryness, sleep problems and mood changes may continue, and can last years after your last period. Many women feel better once hormones settle.

Mindset

Think of it as a new chapter. Many women feel relief from periods and pregnancy worries, so decide what you want this stage to look like.

Lifestyle

Do strength and balance exercise, eat protein, calcium and vitamin D, limit alcohol and avoid smoking. Keep up mammograms, bone density tests and cervical screening as your doctor advises.

See a doctor if: you have ANY bleeding after menopause. This always needs to be checked.

55–60Postmenopause

Estrogen stays low for the rest of life.

What you may notice

Hot flashes fade for many women but continue for some. Vaginal dryness, urinary symptoms and thinning bones may persist, and the risks of heart disease and osteoporosis rise.

Mindset

Stay connected. Friendships, community and purpose support mood and brain health at every age.

Lifestyle

Walk daily, keep up strength and balance work to prevent falls, and eat in a heart-healthy way, for example a Mediterranean-style diet. Keep checking blood pressure, cholesterol and bone density.

See a doctor if: you have back pain or lose height, pelvic pain, new urinary symptoms, or any bleeding.
